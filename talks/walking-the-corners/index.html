Optimality: how do we prove 58 is the best?

Plan feasibility is easy to check

Profit:  \(\OB{9\cdot2 + 8\cdot5 = 58}\).

"Nothing beats it" is harder

Check every corner? Fine here: there are 5.*

A business with 50 products and 50 resources: up to \(\binom{100}{50} \approx 10^{29}\) corners.

We want a short proof that anyone can check.

*\(\binom{5}{2} = 10\) possible corners in our 2D problem, but only 5 are feasible

LPs have a “built in” proof, in the form of an upper bound. Given \(n\) variables and \(m \ge n\) constraints, the best plan sits where \(n\) of the constraints meet, and the proof is built from those same constraints. When a valid upper bound meets a valid lower bound, we are at optimality, and the principle of strong duality guarantees that, whenever there is a best plan, they meet.
Checking a plan = plug in. Optimality needs an argument about ALL plans. Enumeration is hopeless at scale. Last click: duality in one breath. Weak duality = any valid mix gives an upper bound; strong duality = the best bound is tight. The next slides build the dual one step at a time. In the box, m counts the sign constraints x ≥ 0 too: bakery n = 2, m = 5 (matching the C(5, 2) footnote). The best plan (2, 5) sits where oven and labor meet, and the proof is 2.5 × oven + 1.5 × labor.

Duality and bounds*

\(\max\ \OB{9x_1 + 8x_2}\)
  • A feasible solution to our original model (called the primal model) is a lower bound.
    \(9(0) + 8(0) = \OB{0}\)lower bound
    →
    \(9(5) + 8(0) = \OB{45}\)new lower bound
    →
    \(9(4) + 8(2) = \OB{52}\)new lower bound
    →
    \(9(2) + 8(5) = \OB{58}\)new lower bound
  • Any function with coefficients ≥ our objective function's coefficients is ≥ our objective function, for any value of \(x \ge 0\).
    e.g., \(\OB{9}x_1 + \OB{8}x_2 \le 10x_1 + 8x_2\)
  • We can thus use constraints, that are all true for this problem, to bound our objective function from above:
    \(\FL{2x_1 + x_2 \le 10} \quad (\times\, \FL{8}) \quad \Rightarrow \quad 16x_1 + 8x_2 \le 80\)
    \(z = \OB{9x_1 + 8x_2} \le 16x_1 + 8x_2 \le 80\)

* Adapted from A. Majumdar, “Duality: the unsung hero of optimization explained through an example”, Towards Data Science (2021).

Lower bounds come from plans: every feasible plan's profit is at most the best profit, so each better corner raises the lower bound (0, 45, 52, 58; the little plot walks those corners, with the profit line through each, as on the sweep slide; it is the route the simplex method will take later). Upper bounds come from the constraints: they are all true, so any multiple of one (by a number ≥ 0) is true too; 8 × flour = 16x₁ + 8x₂ ≤ 80 has coefficients at least the profit's, and x ≥ 0, so profit ≤ 80. The bar on the right: the best profit is squeezed between the two bounds (58 to 80); the little plot shows both as profit lines, 58 touching the region and 80 (dashed white) beyond it. The next slides tighten the upper bound.

Understanding dual variables

Given our primal model, we can assign a dual variable \(y\) to each constraint

  • Our first dual guess from the previous slide was \(y^\top = (\FL{8}, \OV{0}, \LB{0})\), with \(z \le 80\).
  • Constraint 2 would give us \(y^\top = (\FL{0}, \OV{4}, \LB{0})\): \(z \le 64\); constraint 3, \(y^\top = (\FL{0}, \OV{0}, \LB{9})\): \(z \le 108\). So 64 is our best upper bound so far.
  • We can also take combinations of the constraints, but they need to remain ≥ our coefficient values.
Each constraint gets a multiplier y_i ≥ 0. One constraint alone needs a multiplier big enough to cover BOTH profit coefficients: flour needs max(9/2, 8/1) = 8 (bound 80, from the last slide), oven max(9/3, 8/2) = 4 (bound 64, better: the marker drops), labor max(9/1, 8/2) = 9 (bound 108, worse). Beaten bounds stay on the bar as faint ticks; the best upper bound is now 64. Mixing constraints is allowed as long as every coefficient still covers the profit's; that's the next slide. Worth saying aloud: a "valid dual" is any y ≥ 0 whose mix covers the profit; its bound is b·y.

Primal and dual, in general

Primal
\(\max\)\(\OB{c}^\top x\) \(\text{s.t.}\)\(\textcolor{f2a7c3}{A}x \le \textcolor{9aa0ad}{b}\)\((y)\) \(x \ge 0\)
\(n\) variables, \(m\) constraints
Dual
\(\min\)\(\textcolor{9aa0ad}{b}^\top y\) \(\text{s.t.}\)\(\textcolor{f2a7c3}{A}^\top y \ge \OB{c}\) \(y \ge 0\)
\(m\) variables, \(n\) constraints
  • Transposing swaps rows and columns: \(\textcolor{f2a7c3}{A}\) is \(m \times n\), \(\textcolor{f2a7c3}{A}^\top\) is \(n \times m\).
  • Each column \(j\), times the dual variables, must cover its objective coefficient: \(\sum_i \textcolor{f2a7c3}{a_{ij}}\, y_i \ge \OB{c_j}\) for every \(j\), that is, \(\textcolor{f2a7c3}{A}^\top y \ge \OB{c}\).
  • Every unit of \(y_i\) adds \(\textcolor{9aa0ad}{b_i}\) to the bound: \(\sum_i \textcolor{9aa0ad}{b_i}\, y_i = \textcolor{9aa0ad}{b}^\top y\), and we want the smallest bound.
  • LPs have weak duality: for every feasible \(x\) and \(y\), \(\OB{c}^\top x \le \textcolor{9aa0ad}{b}^\top y\). They also have strong duality: if the primal has an optimal solution, so does the dual, and at optimality \(\OB{c}^\top x = \textcolor{9aa0ad}{b}^\top y\).
The formalizing slide again, in matrix notation; everything is already in place and the bullets name the moves. A is m × n (one row per constraint, one column per variable), Aᵀ is n × m. Column j of A times y must cover c_j, for every j: Aᵀy ≥ c. Each unit of y_i adds b_i to the bound, so the dual minimises bᵀy. One dual variable per primal constraint, one dual constraint per primal variable. Last bullet: weak duality is the bound game (every valid mix caps every plan); strong duality is its last preset, y = (0, 2.5, 1.5) giving 10·0 + 16·2.5 + 12·1.5 = 58 = the best plan. Strong duality needs an optimal solution to exist (not infeasible, not unbounded).

Turning slack into a decision variable (and inequalities into equalities)

Slack is a big part of optimality, and we can turn it into a variable, just like our \(x\)'s

A slack variable \(s_i\) is how much of resource \(i\) is left over:

\(\FL{2x_1 + x_2 + s_1 = 10}\)
\(\OV{3x_1 + 2x_2 + s_2 = 16}\)
\(\LB{x_1 + 2x_2 + s_3 = 12}\)
\(x_1, x_2, s_1, s_2, s_3 \ge 0\)
\(x\) on a line ⇔ that resource is used up ⇔ its slack is 0.
At a corner, two things are zero.

3 equations, 5 unknowns: set 2 to 0, solve for the other 3

  • Set 2 of them to 0 (here \(s_1 = s_2 = 0\): flour and oven are tight), then solve the 3 equations for the other 3 (the basic variables, or the basis; the 2 zeros are nonbasic).
  • \(x_1 = s_2 = 0\) is a corner too, at \((0, 8)\), but solving gives \(s_3 = -4\): labor runs over. Not a feasible corner.
  • Try it yourself: hit clear, then click any 2 boxes to set them to 0; the other 3 get solved for. There are \(\binom{5}{2} = 10\) corners and 5 are feasible. Find them all!

Dual slack: the same trick for the dual (≥ into =)

Each dual constraint, one per product, gets a slack too

A dual slack \(d_j\) is how much the mix over-covers product \(j\)'s profit:

\(\FL{2y_1} + \OV{3y_2} + \LB{y_3} - d_1 = \OB{9}\)bread
\(\FL{y_1} + \OV{2y_2} + \LB{2y_3} - d_2 = \OB{8}\)cake
\(y_1, y_2, y_3, d_1, d_2 \ge 0\)
At each corner, \(y\) is fixed by its two tight constraints: \(y^\top = c_B^\top B^{-1}\) (\(B\): the columns of the corner's basic variables, its basis; \(c_B\): their profits).
Inside the region the primal is valid; outside, the dual can be.

Each reduction uses the other side's answer as its row multipliers: −9 −8 | 0 plus 2.5·oven plus 1.5·labor gives 0 0 | 58; −16 −12 | 0 plus 2·(bread row) plus 5·(cake row) gives 0 0 | 58. Primal and dual variables and slacks at a corner point

Every primal variable has a dual partner: \(x_j\) with its dual slack \(d_j\), \(s_i\) with its dual variable \(y_i\)

  • Primal: the corner's 2 tight constraints give 2 zeros (a slack, or an \(x\) on its axis); the other 3 are solved for: the basis.
  • Dual: the partners of those 3 are 0; the other 2 are solved for: the multipliers of the 2 tight constraints (the dual's basis).
  • In every pair at least one is 0: complementary slackness. The corner is optimal when both rows are \(\ge 0\).
The pairs line up as on the next slides' x and y vectors: x₁–d₁, x₂–d₂, s₁–y₁, s₂–y₂, s₃–y₃. At a corner, the 2 tight constraints are the primal's zeros (s_i = 0 on a resource line, x_j = 0 on an axis); the other 3 primal variables are the basis. In the dual it flips: the partners of the basis are 0, and the 2 partners of the tight constraints are solved for. They are the multipliers of the tight constraints' normals that add up to c (slide 16's "two readings"), labelled on the tight lines. So every pair has a 0 (complementary slackness), and the corner is optimal exactly when both rows are ≥ 0 (primal feasible and dual feasible). Clicks: the three bullets at (0, 0), then (5, 0), (4, 2) (y₁ = −6: not optimal), (0, 6) (d₁ = −5: not optimal), (2, 5) (both rows ≥ 0: optimal). Any corner on the plot can be clicked.

Common names and interpretations for parts of the dual

Dual variable = shadow price

\(\FL{y_1}, \OV{y_2}, \LB{y_3}\): what one more unit of each resource is worth. At the optimum, \(y = (\FL{0}, \OV{2.5}, \LB{1.5})\).

Best profit = best bound = \(\FL{10y_1} + \OV{16y_2} + \LB{12y_3}\), so one more oven hour adds \(\OV{y_2}\), as long as the same dual stays best: 17 hours ⇒ 58 → 60.5, that's \(+\OV{2.5}\). One more unit of flour ⇒ +0 (we already have spare).

Dual slack = reduced cost

Each product has a dual constraint: the value of the resources it uses must cover its profit (bread: \(\FL{2y_1} + \OV{3y_2} + \LB{y_3} \ge \OB{9}\)). Its slack is the product's reduced cost: value of the resources it uses − its profit.

Negative ⇒ the prices undervalue it ⇒ worth making more. At \((0, 0)\) every price is 0, so \(d_1 = -9\) and \(d_2 = -8\): both are worth making.

Convention used throughout: reduced cost d_j = y·a_j − c_j (the dual slack). Optimal ⇔ every dual slack ≥ 0 and every y ≥ 0. Negative = a violated dual constraint = a candidate to enter. Shadow-price check: with 17 oven hours the optimum moves to (2.5, 4.75), profit 60.5.

The simplex method, in one picture

The best plan is always at a corner, so walk from corner to corner

  1. Start at a corner.
  2. Look along the edges. Which ones improve profits? (duals)
  3. Walk one to the next corner (profit never goes down). Repeat.
  4. Stop when no edge improves profits.
There was a shorter way (2 pivots vs. 3 pivots). Which edge to take is the pivot rule, and it's the question this talk builds up to.
The whole algorithm in one picture, before the bookkeeping. "(duals)" on step 2: the primal values alone don't say which edges improve profit; that is read off the whole dual: for an edge leaving an axis (an x enters) its dual slack d_j = yᵀa_j − c_j, for an edge leaving a constraint line (a slack enters) the dual variable y_i itself (negative ⇒ improving); the tableau's bottom row holds both. Step 4's "stop" is the dual becoming feasible (every d_j ≥ 0 and y_i ≥ 0). Step 3, "profit never goes down": along the chosen edge profit rises at rate |d_q|, and the ratio test picks a step t ≥ 0 that stays feasible, so each pivot changes profit by |d_q|·t ≥ 0 (0 → 45 → 52 → 58 here). It's ≥, not >: at a degenerate corner t = 0 and the pivot changes the basis without moving (where stalling and cycling, and Bland's rule, come in). The dual's bound yᵀb equals the current profit at every corner but is only a valid upper bound once the dual is feasible, at the end. the next slides write it down with bases (revised simplex) and the tableau, whose bottom row shows "which edges improve" and "stop". Step 2 walks the 3-pivot route (via bread, the same corners whose profits were the lower bounds 0, 45, 52, 58); step 4 shows the 2-pivot route via cake.

Putting it all together: how simplex works

Relax dual feasibility. Go from corner point to feasible corner point, guided by dual variable values towards dual feasibility.

This is essentially the revised simplex method. Real solvers skip \(D\) and reuse \(B\): \(y^\top = c_B^\top B^{-1}\), because \(B^{-1}\) already holds what \(D\) needs. They also update \(B\)'s factorization at each pivot instead of rebuilding it: much cheaper on large problems.
Revised simplex on the bakery, Dantzig's rule, from the origin. Iteration 1 one formula per click: the basis B (slacks: B = I); primal B x_B = b gives x_B = (10, 16, 12), the corner (0, 0); dual: D = −I (d₁, d₂ are the dual basics) gives d = (−9, −8), with y = 0 fixed by the basis; pricing d_j = yᵀa_j − c_j on the nonbasic columns gives d = (−9, −8): not optimal; x₁ enters (most negative); direction u = B⁻¹a₁ = (2, 3, 1): x₁'s column written in terms of the basis, i.e. each unit of x₁ uses up 2 of s₁, 3 of s₂ and 1 of s₃; ratio test x_B ÷ u over the positive u: min(5, 5.33, 12) = 5, so s₁ is the first to hit 0 and leaves; update B and walk to (5, 0). Every later iteration is built the same way, one formula per click: at (5, 0) y = (4.5, 0, 0), x₂ enters (−3.5), s₂ leaves → (4, 2); at (4, 2) y = (−6, 7, 0), so d_{s₁} = −6: s₁ enters, s₃ leaves → (2, 5); at (2, 5) y = (0, 2.5, 1.5), every d ≥ 0: optimal, cᵀx = bᵀy = 58. On screen: A with its variables above the columns, the vectors x and c (one entry per variable), y (5 long like x and row-aligned with it: d₁, d₂, y₁, y₂, y₃, each next to its primal partner x₁, x₂, s₁, s₂, s₃; it is the bottom row yᵀA − c over all 5 columns. The partners of the 3 basic variables are 0 by choice (complementary slackness) and appear with the basis; the dual's other 2 are solved, y at the dual step, d at pricing. the dual's own basis D (2 × 2) = the columns of [Aᵀ | −I] for the 2 dual basics, i.e. the normals of the 2 tight constraints, e.g. [−1 0; 0 −1] at (0, 0); the dual step solves D·(dual basics) = (9, 8). Sanity check / the reuse: B and D share one core block K = tight resource rows × basic product columns (B contains K, B⁻¹ contains K⁻¹, D = Kᵀ plus −I columns for the d's), so the textbook shortcut Bᵀy = c_B, i.e. yᵀ = c_Bᵀ B⁻¹, gives the same y without a second factorization; e.g. at (4, 2) K = [2 1; 3 2], D = [2 3; 1 2]), b (lined up with A's rows), then B and B⁻¹ written out for the current basis. Basic columns of A and basic entries of x and c are tinted (that is B, x_B, c_B); entering gold, leaving red; the brackets of the vectors a step uses turn gold. Last click: the note that this is essentially revised simplex; real solvers reuse B (yᵀ = c_Bᵀ B⁻¹) and update a factorization of B rather than building D, which is far cheaper for large problems.

Pivoting: enter, walk the edge, leave, repeat

One step per click: entering variable, min ratio test, exiting variable, pivot

Same tableau, same place as the last slide; the equations make way for the min ratio column. Each iteration takes four clicks. Entering: any negative bottom-row entry can enter (raising it raises the profit); Dantzig's rule is greedy: the most negative, the biggest gain per unit, not necessarily the biggest step. Min ratio test: value ÷ entry for the positive entries of the entering column (how far it can grow before a basic variable hits 0). Exiting: the smallest ratio's row. Pivot: row operations turn the entering column into the identity column the leaving variable had. (0,0): bread enters (−9), flour runs out at 5 (oven 5.33), s₁ leaves → (5,0), 45. (5,0): cake enters (−3.5), oven runs out (ratio 2), s₂ leaves → (4,2), 52. (4,2): s₁ enters (−6, flour's shadow price is negative: stop using all the flour); the x₂ row's −3 doesn't limit it; labor runs out (ratio 1), s₃ leaves → (2,5), 58, optimal. Last click: Dantzig took 3 pivots. What if we had entered cake (−8) instead? Next slide.

What if we chose the other direction?

Cake (−8) is negative too, so it can enter first: same steps, a different route

The same four clicks per iteration, entering cake first. (0,0): x₂ enters (−8); ratios 10/1 = 10, 16/2 = 8, 12/2 = 6: labor runs out first (oven at 8, flour at 10) ⇒ s₃ leaves → (0, 6), profit 48. (0,6): bread's reduced cost is now −5 (labor is priced at 4, a batch of bread uses 1 hour: 4 − 9); x₁ enters; ratios 4/1.5 = 2.67, 4/2 = 2, 6/0.5 = 12: oven runs out ⇒ s₂ leaves → (2, 5), 58, optimal. 2 pivots instead of Dantzig's 3: the greedy choice isn't always the shortest route. Which negative entry to pick is the pivot rule; that's the next part. Last click shows Dantzig's route dashed for comparison.

2 pivots or 3: does it matter?

Not for our bakery. But real problems are bigger, often integer, and rarely start at a convenient corner.

Bigger problems

thousands to millions

Real LPs (airline crews, power grids, supply chains) have thousands to millions of variables and constraints. Each pivot scales with the increased problem size.

Integer problems

an LP at every node

Whole-number decisions (appointment slots, how many trucks) are solved by branch and bound, i.e., many LPs per problem, meaning a few extra pivots per LP add up fast.

Harder starts

no easy first corner

In our bakery, zero product is feasible. "At least" or "exactly" constraints (bake at least 20 batches, staff exactly 8 hours) would require simplex to run an additional phase to find a feasible start.

Even one more dimension on this toy problem changes the landscape. Next: a third product.
Bridge from the 2D walk to the rest of the talk. One pivot doesn't matter for the bakery; it matters when (1) problems are large (each pivot is a linear solve; pivot counts grow), (2) problems are integer (branch and bound solves thousands of LPs, each warm-started from its parent's basis, so savings multiply), (3) the start is hard (≥ or = constraints make the origin infeasible; Phase 1 finds a first feasible corner, often with artificial variables). The 3D dual: three planes meet at the corner

\(2 \times (\FL{2x_1 + x_2 + x_3 \le 10})\)
\(1 \times (\OV{3x_1 + 2x_2 + x_3 \le 16})\)
\(2 \times (\LB{x_1 + 2x_2 + x_3 \le 12})\)
\(= 9x_1 + 8x_2 + 5x_3 \le 60\)

That is exactly the profit \(\OB{9x_1 + 8x_2 + 5x_3}\), so no plan beats 60, and \((2, 4, 2)\) earns 60.

Three tight planes, three unknowns ⇒ one corner. The profit arrow lies inside the cone of the planes' outward directions ⇔ every multiplier ≥ 0 ⇔ optimal.

(In other words, the objective direction is a nonnegative combination of the three tight constraints, so the corner is primal and dual feasible ⇔ optimal.)

The multipliers 2, 1, 2 are the new shadow prices (bottom row of the last tableau). Same story as the 2D dual, one dimension up: the corner is where the three planes used in the dual meet. Why "in the cone" means optimal: each plane's outward arrow is its coefficients (flour (2, 1, 1): stepping that way uses more flour); the cone is everything you can build from the three arrows with amounts ≥ 0. The stack is 2·(2, 1, 1) + 1·(3, 2, 1) + 2·(1, 2, 1) = (9, 8, 5), the profit arrow, so "in the cone" = "a valid mix in the bound game". Walls push, they don't pull: if the profit's pull can be balanced by the walls pushing (weights ≥ 0) the point is stuck: optimal; if balancing it needs a wall to pull (a negative weight), the point slides off that wall along an edge and profit rises (slide 16's (4, 2): y₁ = −6). One line: any move Δ that stays feasible has aᵢ·Δ ≤ 0 for each tight plane, so c·Δ = 2a₁·Δ + 1a₂·Δ + 2a₃·Δ ≤ 0. "Nonnegative", not "convex": a convex combination's weights also sum to 1 (these sum to 5).

Dantzig visits all 8 corners (7 pivots); steepest edge goes straight to the top (1 pivot). The same construction in n dimensions makes Dantzig take \(2^n - 1\) pivots: about \(10^{15}\) in 50 dimensions. (Bland: 5 here.)
Klee and Minty (1972). The cube is squashed so each step of Dantzig's tour improves the profit a little: z = 0, 20, 30, 50, 75, 95, 105, 125. Every rule above has its own bad examples, so no simple rule is fast on every LP; that is the open question behind the RL work.

Two more things about rules

Devex

Steepest edge needs the length of every candidate edge at every pivot, which is expensive on large problems. Devex keeps cheap running estimates of those lengths instead.

Real solvers use Devex or steepest edge. On the bakery, Devex makes the same choices as Bland.

Bland's rule and cycling

At a degenerate corner (more constraints tight than needed), a pivot can change the basis without moving. A careless rule can then cycle through the same bases forever.

Bland's rule provably never cycles. It is slow in practice, so it is mostly a safety net; solvers also break ties with small perturbations.

Inside SimplexRL: a network that learns to pivot in progress

Our current model, walked through on the bakeryFelix Parker, Emmett Springer, Danielle Ripsman, Kimia Ghobadi

1 · Read the LP once per problem

Variables and constraints become nodes; each nonzero of \(A\) is an edge (thicker = bigger coefficient).

A graph neural network passes messages along the edges; each coloured strip is a node's vector (embedding).

2 · Compare the candidates every pivot

Each candidate (negative reduced cost) adds the solver's numbers; a transformer compares candidates with each other and with the current basis.

3 · Pick

The solver pivots on the top score. Dantzig takes bread: 3 pivots. Cake first: 2.

How it learns
Imitate a classic rule, or A*
Reinforcement learning: −1 per pivot
Search as a teacher (like AlphaZero): in training, try pivots ahead in the solver and teach the network the best one
The early learned offline policy (IQL) already ties steepest edge on the 40 × 40 LP.
Our model, on the bakery. (1) The LP becomes a graph: a node for each variable and each constraint, an edge for each nonzero coefficient. A graph neural network passes messages along the edges, so every node ends up with a vector (the coloured strip) describing its place in the whole LP. This happens once per problem. (2) At every pivot, each candidate (negative reduced cost) adds the solver's numbers, and a transformer compares the candidates with each other and with the current basis. (3) The model scores each candidate and the solver pivots on the top one. The scores here are illustrative: the point is that a learned rule can prefer cake (2 pivots) over Dantzig's bread (3). How it learns: copy a classic rule or A*; reinforcement learning, where every pivot costs −1; and search as a teacher, trying pivots ahead during training (like AlphaZero). Result so far: the early offline policy (IQL) ties steepest edge on the 40 × 40 LP, 13 pivots.

Takeaways

The best plan sits at a corner (or on a tied edge or face, whose corners are all best too).
Shadow prices are dual variables: adding up the tight constraints with them proves a plan optimal.
Reduced costs are dual slacks: a negative one says the dual doesn't work yet, and which way to move.
A pivot rule is actually just a policy that we can decide. The 'best' one can vary problem to problem, potentially creating or removing exponential amounts of work.
